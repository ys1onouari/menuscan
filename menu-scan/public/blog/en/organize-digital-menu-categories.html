How to Organize Product Categories in a Digital Menu

October 3, 2026 · 9 min

A practical method for grouping and ordering digital menu categories so customers can find products quickly on a smartphone.

A digital menu can contain dozens of products, but customers should not have to scroll through one long list to understand what you offer. The way you group and order products directly affects how easily a customer can find a dish, drink, pastry or other item on a smartphone.

The challenge is to create enough categories to make the menu easy to scan without creating so many sections that the customer has to open several categories before finding what they want. The right structure depends on your activity, your number of products, your customers and the way your offer is organized.

Start with the way customers think about your offer

Before creating categories, list all the products that need to appear in the digital menu. Then look for natural groups. A category should answer a simple question: what type of products would a customer expect to find together?

For example, a café may separate hot drinks, cold drinks, breakfast items and desserts. A pastry shop may use categories such as pastries, cakes, individual desserts and drinks. A food truck may organize its offer around burgers, sandwiches, sides and drinks. These structures are different because the products and the way customers choose them are different.

Avoid creating categories simply because products have different names. The goal is not to classify every item in detail. The goal is to help customers understand the offer quickly.

Group products by a clear characteristic

Useful categories usually have an obvious relationship between their products. Depending on the establishment, you can group items by product type, meal occasion, preparation style or another distinction that customers can understand without explanation.

  • Restaurant: starters, main courses, grills, desserts and drinks can be appropriate when these groups reflect the actual offer.
  • Café or tea room: coffees, teas, cold drinks, breakfast items, pastries and desserts may provide a natural structure.
  • Pastry shop: pastries, cakes, cookies, individual desserts and beverages can separate products clearly.
  • Food truck: sandwiches, burgers, sides, sauces and drinks may be easier to scan than a category for every product family.
  • Hotel or riad: breakfast, snacks, main dishes, desserts and beverages can be used when the digital menu covers food service for guests.
  • Shop or grocery store: products can be organized by families that make sense for the catalogue, such as beverages, snacks, pantry products or other relevant groups.

How many products should a category contain?

There is no single number that works for every digital menu. A category becomes difficult to use when it contains a long, undifferentiated list. On the other hand, splitting a small group into many tiny categories can make navigation unnecessarily complicated.

Consider a café with a small selection of hot drinks. Creating separate categories for espresso, milk-based coffee and tea may be unnecessary if the customer can understand the entire selection at a glance. In a larger restaurant, however, separating starters, salads, main courses and desserts may make the menu much easier to navigate.

A useful test is to ask whether each category helps a customer make a decision. If two categories are almost indistinguishable, combining them may produce a clearer menu. If one category contains very different products, dividing it may make browsing easier.

Signs that a category is too broad

  • It contains several unrelated product families.
  • Customers need to scroll through many items before finding a relevant product.
  • The category name is so general that it does not explain what is inside.
  • Products that customers would normally search for separately are mixed together.

Signs that a category is too detailed

  • Several categories contain only one or two products.
  • Customers have to move between many sections to compare similar items.
  • Category names are difficult to understand at a glance.
  • The menu structure becomes longer than the product list itself.

Choose the category order carefully

Even well-defined categories can become less useful if they appear in an inconvenient order. The first sections should normally help customers understand the main offer rather than simply follow the order in which products were entered.

Think about the customer journey. If your establishment is known for a particular type of product, that category may deserve a prominent position. If customers commonly start with breakfast, lunch or drinks, the order can reflect that use case. The appropriate sequence depends on your activity and customer profile.

For a restaurant, a possible structure could begin with starters, continue with main dishes, then desserts and drinks. Another restaurant might logically place grills or signature dishes earlier because they form a central part of its offer. There is no universal order that every establishment should copy.

For a café, drinks may come first, followed by breakfast items, snacks and desserts. For a pastry shop, pastries and individual desserts may be more relevant at the beginning of the menu. For a hotel or riad, the order can depend on whether the menu is intended mainly for breakfast, room service or another food offering.

Keep categories simple on a smartphone

A digital menu is often consulted from a phone, so the category structure should remain easy to scan on a small screen. Customers should be able to understand the main sections without having to navigate through an excessive number of choices.

Use short, familiar category names. A customer should not need to interpret internal terminology used by your kitchen or purchasing team. If your staff calls a group of products one thing but customers know it by another name, the customer-facing category should use the clearer wording.

Descriptions also matter, but they should not turn every category into a block of text. The category name provides orientation; the individual product name, description and price provide the necessary detail.

Consider the complete path

  1. The customer opens the digital menu.
  2. They identify the relevant category.
  3. They scan the products in that category.
  4. They read the description when they need more information.
  5. They check the price before choosing.

If a customer needs to open several unrelated sections to compare basic options, the structure may need simplification.

Examples for different businesses in Morocco

The same organization method should not be imposed on every business. An establishment in a tourist area may serve an international clientele, while another business may mainly serve local customers. The number of products and frequency of menu changes can also differ.

Restaurant

A restaurant with a broad food offer could use categories such as starters, salads, main dishes, grilled items, desserts and drinks. If the restaurant has a smaller menu, combining related sections may make browsing faster.

Café or salon de thé

A café might organize its menu around hot drinks, cold drinks, breakfast, snacks and desserts. A salon de thé with a particularly varied tea selection could create a dedicated tea category while keeping other sections compact.

Pastry shop

A pastry shop can separate products according to how customers usually choose them: individual pastries, cakes, cookies, desserts and drinks, for example. If certain categories contain very few products, they can be grouped where this remains clear.

Food truck

A food truck usually benefits from a compact structure because customers often need to understand the offer quickly. A few clear sections such as sandwiches, sides and drinks may be easier to use than many specialized categories.

Hotel or riad

A hotel or riad can structure its digital food menu around the service being presented. Depending on the offer, categories might include breakfast, snacks, main dishes, desserts and beverages. The structure should reflect what guests actually need to choose.

A practical checklist before publishing

Before putting your digital menu online, review the categories as if you were a first-time customer.

  • Every product has a logical place: no item should appear in an arbitrary category simply to complete the structure.
  • Category names are immediately understandable: avoid internal terms and unnecessary abbreviations.
  • Related products stay together: customers should not have to search several sections for comparable items.
  • The category order reflects your offer: place the sections in an order that makes sense for your customers and activity.
  • There are no unnecessary categories: merge very small or nearly identical sections when appropriate.
  • Broad categories are still manageable: divide them when they contain clearly different product families.
  • The menu works on a phone: check that the structure remains easy to scan on a small screen.
  • Prices and product information are consistent: use the same presentation logic throughout the menu.
  • Unavailable products are reviewed: remove or update products that are no longer part of the current offer.
  • The structure matches your current business: a menu should evolve when your products, categories or customer needs change.

Common category mistakes to avoid

One frequent mistake is copying the structure of another establishment. A category system that works for a large restaurant may be unnecessarily complex for a small café or food truck.

Another mistake is creating categories based only on internal organization. Customers do not necessarily know how your products are grouped behind the counter or in the kitchen. The digital menu should reflect their way of understanding the offer.

It is also easy to add a new category every time a new product family appears. Over time, this can create a fragmented menu. Before adding a section, check whether the product can logically fit into an existing category.

Finally, avoid putting everything into one large category simply because you want to keep the menu short. Fewer categories do not automatically mean easier navigation. A clear structure is usually more useful than an artificially small number of sections.

What to prepare before organizing your digital menu

Start with a complete list of your current products, including their categories, prices and relevant descriptions. Then review the list from the customer perspective and decide which products naturally belong together.

If your offer changes regularly, keep the structure flexible enough to accommodate new products without creating unnecessary categories. When prices, products or offers change, the menu structure should continue to reflect the current offer rather than an older version of the catalogue.

For establishments serving several types of customers, you can also consider whether different languages are useful. In Morocco, this may be relevant according to your location, activity and clientele. The objective is to keep the same logical category structure understandable across the languages you choose to present.

A well-organized digital menu does not need a complicated hierarchy. It needs categories that customers recognize, an order that matches the way they choose products, and enough detail to distinguish items without overwhelming the mobile screen.
